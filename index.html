Ooloi Labs · Open Knowledge Framework

Platform Builder

A configuration suite that lets non-technical teams build, govern and personalise an entire platform, without filing a ticket.

RoleProduct Designer: research, UX, interaction and visual design
Team2 designers, with strategy and development
TimelineAbout 3 months: 3 weeks of design and decisions, then development of the configuration system
SettingStage-gate system
Context

Every change to the platform went through the developers.

The Open Knowledge Framework is a modular platform for research, learning management, data collection and visualisation. It was powerful, but every act of configuration, from a new content template to new languages, permissions or a redesigned homepage, needed a developer.

As it scaled across clients, the dev team was buried in customisation tickets while trying to ship features. Product owners waited in line to change things they understood better than anyone.

Problem

A platform only its builders could operate.

Developers stretched thin

The team was rebuilding the backend and frontend while fielding a steady stream of one-off configuration requests.

Control in the wrong hands

Product owners and admins knew their tenants, content and users best, but had no direct way to act on that knowledge.

A dashboard that couldn't carry the weight

The old admin dashboard had shallow, hard-to-read structure, with navigation and analytics bolted onto the header. Fixing it meant restructuring the information, not restyling it.

Approach

We audited the system before designing a new one.

We listed every function the existing dashboard exposed, then interviewed the development and strategy teams about what customising the platform really took: the tasks, the skills, and how much control each role needed. Two questions ran through all of it: what should be client-facing, and who gets to control what.

We also studied CRM, ERP and data-management products, where many connected services are governed from one interface. That research became the backbone of a new information architecture: a map of every function that could safely move from developers to admins.

The decision that shaped everything

Instead of keeping the admin dashboard as a separate surface, we folded it into the Platform Builder: one suite, one mental model, seven areas to govern.

Before: two dashboards

Control was split between an Admin dashboard and a Super Admin dashboard, so the same kind of setting lived in different places depending on who was allowed to change it.

After: one Platform Builder, seven areas

We restructured everything into seven areas: user settings, content templates, tags manager, system, global configuration, page builder and learning modules. Roles now decide access within each area instead of deciding which dashboard you open.

The build

Seven areas, one way of working.

Each area was designed so a non-technical admin could run it with confidence, and every one follows the same pattern, which makes the suite learnable instead of a set of separate tools.

Content templates

Admins start from a list of every template on the platform and create new ones for each use case.

The Template Creation Interface

The heart of the build. Admins compose forms and pages from blocks and sections, dragging, repositioning and nesting components in a side panel while a live preview updates beside it. Template-making stopped being a developer task.

Adding a block always takes the same four steps:

01Select a componentPick from the component library.
02Choose a variantEach shows what it's best used for.
03Describe itLabel, description, rules and meta information.
04SaveSee the change on the template.

Tags and collection pages

Admins assemble the pages that group tagged content: a cover, a description and tabs for related material, with parts added or removed as needed.

System settings

Detailed control over who can do what, how the site is navigated and how it looks.

Global configuration and users

Authentication, feature toggles and complex settings moved out of scattered places into one panel. A single user dashboard handles adding people one at a time or in bulk, assigning roles and exporting records.

Outcome

The people closest to the work stopped asking permission.

We tested each area first with the strategy and development teams, then with every client and tenant owner, until using it felt natural. Then we handed it to the Strategy and Planning team.

Within a week of onboarding, admins who had never written code were creating templates, changing features and editing access control themselves.

What used to need a ticket and a wait now took a few clicks and an afternoon.

43%fewer task and ad-hoc requests to engineering
7areas of the platform handed to admins
"A good system doesn't just do the work; it redistributes the ability to do the work."
Reflection

What I took from it

The hard part is restraint.

Every area had to be powerful enough to be useful and constrained enough to be safe in non-technical hands. That tension between control and guardrails is the real design problem in any self-serve tool.

What I'd work on next

How the suite holds up beyond seven areas, opinionated defaults so admins don't face a blank canvas, and previews that show what a configuration change will affect before it ships.

Annotation System

Field researchers tag and sort interview notes as they write them, instead of days later at a desk.

RoleProduct Designer: research, UX, interaction and visual design
TeamSheneille Patil, Harsh Vatsa
Timeline6 weeks
SettingFeature request, agile design sprint
Context

Collecting field notes was solved. Making sense of them wasn't.

The Open Knowledge Framework (now DOTS) gives NGO field researchers forms to collect and transcribe interviews, across many kinds of social-impact work. What they still lacked was a way to label and synthesise what they collected.

Problem

Every step between the interview and the report added time.

Researchers transcribed, structured, categorised and then layered in their insights, and every feedback round on the analysis made it longer. On large field studies they needed to tag and sort inside the app, as they worked.

Research

We started with how researchers already work.

Three questions guided us:

  • How does an interviewer record and detail data in the field?
  • Once notes and transcripts are written, how do they label and categorise them?
  • How do insights travel from raw notes to a presentation, and could labelling happen right away instead of days later?

We interviewed 20 interviewers working on programmes in sexual health, reproductive rights, watershed management and social justice. We also looked at how Google Docs, Medium and Notion handle annotation, and which ideas were worth borrowing.

What we heard

Designing the flow

Built around the interviewer's convenience, not the database's.

We sketched user flows, moved into low-fidelity wireframes, ran a heuristic review and tried several tagging approaches before settling on one tuned to the research template. The core flow has four steps:

01Open a templateStart from the team's notes template.
02Highlight textSelect the passage that matters.
03Choose a tagPick a category, or create a tag in it.
04SaveThe tag is stored against the selection.

Mobile explorations

Mobile explorations of three different flows for adding a tag to a note: creating a new one, searching for one, and choosing from a preset list.

Creating a tag

A docked field above the keyboard filters existing tags as you type and offers “Create” inline when nothing matches, so making a new tag never leaves the flow.

Searching for a tag

A dedicated full-screen picker with its own search field, so finding the right tag in a long list takes a couple of keystrokes.

Choosing from preset tags

A bottom sheet keeps the passage in view while you pick a category, choose up to five options and apply them in one go.

The design

Two modes: first you capture, then you make sense of it.

On desktop: highlight a passage, choose Annotate, pick a category and its attributes, and apply. Hover a tagged passage to review its attributes.

Raw notes mode

Interviewers write notes and transcripts, highlight a passage and open the tag menu. Choosing a tag assigns it to the selection, and they can create a new tag inside any category, so the taxonomy grows with the research. Tagged text is highlighted straight away, and hovering it shows its attributes.

Synthesis mode

Flip the switch and every tagged excerpt gathers under its attribute, next to the researcher's own note on it, with the categories listed down the side. A page of raw notes becomes a structured document, ready to publish, without re-entering anything.

Outcome

Adopted in the pilot, then taken to three more countries.

After internal testing, the tool launched in a pilot project in Nairobi.

Notes were annotated and processed faster, so other researchers could spot shared patterns and understand local customs sooner.

85%of pilot researchers adopted the new method
3more countries now use the feature and template
20interviewers shaped the design
Reflection

What I took from it

Good interaction design disappears into an existing habit.

We didn't replace the researchers' way of taking notes. We digitised it and gave it structure, and that is why adoption held.

The notebook that stayed is the next problem.

Many researchers still wrote by hand. Next I'd make on-device capture as easy as a pen, and add tag governance so user-created tags don't fragment across teams.

OKF Design System

A scalable, accessible component library for a white-label product, built so a young start-up could move fast without its screens drifting apart.

RoleProduct Designer: component architecture, style system, accessibility, documentation
TeamMe, Sheneille Patil
ToolsFigma, Notion, Storybook.js
TimelineBuilt 2019–2022, then updates and documentation
Context

A young start-up needed one way to build its product.

I joined Ooloi Labs in 2019, straight out of architecture school, as an interface and experience design intern. The team had a prototype and user flows, but no shared system to guide design and development.

We built one from scratch between 2019 and 2022, carrying the founders' standards, values and philosophy into the new product framework. It became my longest-running project at the firm. After 2022 the work moved to updates and documentation, kept in Figma, Notion and Storybook.

Problem

Small errors compound in a design library.

A new team, many collaborators, unusual use cases and handoffs that lost detail. Even small slips made final screens look like a different product, which hurt the brand and the user experience. The risk grows as a design scales across platforms and clients, which for a white-label product is the whole point.

  • One source for components, patterns and styles
  • Simple enough for cross-functional teams in fast development cycles
  • One recognisable design language across every product line
  • Less load on a small design team: no recreating the same component twice
Approach

Start from the smallest parts, built for real screens.

We used Brad Frost's Atomic Design to plan and sort the library, from atoms up to full pages. Buttons, text inputs and tags came first, then components like cards and button groups, then templates. Existing mock-ups and wireframes told us what to build next.

The library had to embody the brand: transparency, impact orientation, approachability, simplicity and groundedness. Every component was documented with its anatomy and variations so developers read from the same source as designers.

Style guide

A full style system, agreed with developers.

Colour

Simple, friendly and accessible: basic, primary, grey, alert, chart and hover colours, each named for its role.

Grids

After several rounds with the development team we set grids and gutters for five widths, from small (up to 600px) to XX-large (up to 1600px).

Typography

Noto Sans and Noto Serif on a responsive scale. Each style is named Serif_X_Y or Sans_X_Y: X is the size on screens under 600px, Y the size above, so developers never had to guess.

Icons, illustrations and patterns

We adopted the Phosphor icon library, and worked with an in-house illustrator on empty-state, error and task-complete illustrations and a set of abstract placeholder patterns.

1 / 5
Components

A library that grows one use case at a time.

We started with what the product needed then and added components as new use cases arrived, designing each one against the existing set. Every component ships in light and dark versions.

Accessibility

Accessibility was part of the design, not a check at the end.

We tested every text and component colour against its backgrounds with the WebAIM contrast checker, and let the ratios decide which combinations people with low vision could read best.

ContentAAAAA
Normal text4.5 : 17 : 1
Large text3 : 14.5 : 1
UI components3 : 1n/a
Reflection

What I took from it

Designers and developers built from the same components, every product line shared one brand, and the design team stopped recreating the same elements.

"It didn't produce a screen anyone would praise. It produced the conditions under which every screen could stay coherent."

The most valuable work was infrastructure.

Designing for other designers and developers, and documenting decisions so they survive handoffs, mattered as much as any component I drew.

A design system is never finished.

The real test is whether it still holds fifty components later. Next time I'd invest even more in naming and structure, so growth doesn't turn into entropy.

Chalkpiece design challenge · 2020

Pally

A mobile platform that helps young adults look after their mental well-being through community and structure.

RoleProduct Designer
TeamMe, Theertha Shetty
Timeline1 week, 10–17 Oct 2020
ToolsFigma, Miro
Context

Coping with stress during the pandemic.

Chalkpiece, a non-profit, set this challenge in 2020. Young adults were facing anxiety, stress and depression, with pressure to succeed academically, socially and professionally. Affordable care was hard to reach, and the stigma around asking for help kept many from seeking it at all.

We set out to build an app where people could seek guidance, share experiences and reach professionals, with structure to help them plan and prioritise through a hard year.

Who it serves

Students and young professionals

Who supports them

Therapists, counsellors and mental health coaches

How

Community, goals and gentle structure

Research

58 people told us what was weighing on them.

We followed the Double Diamond. Secondary research covered articles and surveys on young adults' mental health during COVID-19. We surveyed 58 people about their stressors, habits, awareness and goals, then interviewed 5 of them in depth.

Four needs came out of it:

  1. Track your mood to encourage self-reflection
  2. Share and connect in a supportive community
  3. Reach your best self with professional support and resources
  4. Set goals and track the tasks that build a growth mindset
Persona

Designing for Malini, 26, an architect.

A persona and empathy map kept the team focused on one person's needs, goals and frustrations. We mapped Malini's typical day before and with Pally, then grouped everything we'd heard into four themes with an affinity map.

The Pally assistant will

Suggest ways to reach a goal, suggest content based on mood, watch for crisis triggers, send gentle reminders and encourage people to engage with each other.

Designing

From structure to a warm, calm brand.

We mapped the information architecture across onboarding and two levels of navigation, sketched low-fidelity wireframes, then gave Pally a warm palette and friendly type so it felt light rather than clinical.

Final screens

What Malini sees.

Onboarding records goals and interests so the home feed, quotes, chatrooms and suggested professionals are personal from day one. The home screen shows today's mood, goals and inspiration at a glance.

Three flows in motion: picking goals during onboarding, sharing a post with the community, and switching between one-to-one chats and themed chatrooms for finance, work-life balance, fitness, books and more.

1 / 3
Testing

Four people, four tasks, four changes.

We asked four people from our audience to journal their feelings, message a chatroom, add a goal and explore the community. They moved through the app easily and liked the interface. They also told us what to fix:

  1. Rename "Pals" to "Feed", the word they already know from other apps.
  2. Make the mood colours clearer; the colour coding confused people.
  3. Keep either claps or likes, not both.
  4. Move the journal's entry-type dropdown above the text, and separate journalling from community posts.

Public Policy Lab

Reimagining civic design to make room for future generations, without slowing down the work happening today.

RoleResearcher and strategist: research, facilitation, workshop design
TeamStudio team of 14, working across the different solutions
Timeline14 weeks, 2025
SettingPublic Policy Lab, New York
The ask

Embed long-term thinking without overloading the teams.

Public Policy Lab runs a structured, ten-step release model that moves projects from scope to scale. It builds momentum, clarity and accountability, but it leaves little room to ask: what happens after we're gone?

"It never feels like there's enough time."

A project lead, echoed across 19 interviews and workshops.

Insight

The future had no shared definition.

One person thought 18 months ahead; another thought in generations. Teams were focused on immediate needs, existing foresight tools felt like extra work, and long-term thinking happened too late or not at all.

So

A rigid futures tool would fail. We designed small moments of future-thinking that fit inside the process PPL already had.

Research

Outside models, inside truth.

We studied the Danish Design Centre, e-Estonia and Helsinki Design Lab, but the sharpest insight came from PPL's own team. Across 19 interviews, shadowing and retrospectives, people sketched their workday, mapped how decisions really happened and revisited past projects through a futures lens.

Prototype 1

What if futures thinking took 20 minutes?

We built scenario workshops around a real problem: ER overcrowding in post-pandemic Baltimore. Five teams planned across three horizons, then adapted as we broke their plans: funding vanished, a new administration arrived, another pandemic wave hit.

01NowImmediate response
02Next1–4 years
03Future5+ years
Prototype 2

Make the future visible.

If futures thinking lives in a report, it disappears. So the canvas is physical, modular and foldable, and stays up in PPL's workspace. Each project adds a panel until the wall becomes an archive of the futures imagined across the lab's work.

01ProjectWhat it is
02ProblemWhat it solves
03TimelineWhen it lands
04Key takeawayWhat we learned
05Desirable futureWhat we're aiming for
Prototype 3

From the future, back to now.

A card game that makes backcasting easy to facilitate. Instead of asking a team what they should think about, the cards give them something specific to react to, challenge and build on.

01Imagine a future
02Identify what had to change
03Work backward through decisions
04Name what can happen now
The system

From an exercise to a process.

riPPL

A lightweight futures toolkit mapped onto the release model, with prompts at natural checkpoints.

PPLay Book

A printed backcasting exercise that uses several lenses to bring staff and partners into longer-term thinking.

Skill and process

Teach people to think in longer timelines, and build that thinking into the work itself.

"Today's solutions are tomorrow's problems."
Reflection

Good facilitation is a design problem.

There are already plenty of futures frameworks. The harder question was whether an overloaded team would actually use one, so every activity had to earn its twenty minutes.

I designed and facilitated the participant workshops, researched case studies on participatory design, supported the futures canvas and wrote content for the card game.

Design for adoption.

A futures practice is only useful when people can pick it up, understand it and use it again after the designers leave.

Understand the power structures.

Organisational design means learning where power sits and where decisions really flow from.

ESKALA × Parsons · Design sprint

ESKALA

Designing microfinance that grows without exploiting the people it serves.

RoleStrategist and systems designer, narrative framing
TeamHarsh Vatsa, Joanna Varghese, Clara Chen
Timeline5-week design sprint, 2025
SettingHonduras, Nicaragua and Panama
Context

What ESKALA does

ESKALA grew out of Global Brigades and now runs as a for-profit impact investor. Instead of lending to farmers directly, it makes micro-equity investments: small ownership stakes in community banking co-ops, called cajas, in Honduras, Nicaragua and Panama.

Each caja decides for itself how to lend that money, mostly to farmers and artisans. Because ESKALA is a shareholder and not a lender, the capital comes without the harsh terms of a loan, and more of the return stays in the community.

How might we

Help ESKALA scale its capital across Honduras, Nicaragua and Panama in a way that builds farmers' knowledge, trust and collective market power, instead of their dependence on ESKALA?

Problem

Access to capital is hard in remote farming communities.

For ESKALA’s capital to work in these communities, three things have to be in place:

Literacy

Financial products are hard to navigate without practical knowledge of credit, budgeting, savings and risk.

Trust

Formal systems feel distant and opaque; informal savings are accessible but rarely open a path to growth.

Market power

Even productive farmers lose value to price swings, distance from markets and unequal bargaining.

68% of Latin Americans have never received personal finance training (World Economic Forum, 2023).

Approach

Every idea had to work for Maria.

Maria is a composite coffee farmer in rural Nicaragua. She was a design constraint, not a research participant: if an idea couldn't survive her conditions, we challenged it. She isn't a substitute for broader evidence.

The system

Turning financial inclusion into a collective system.

Our proposal builds capability first, then collective capacity, then market leverage, while lending between the institution and individuals carries on.

01LearnStorytelling, roleplay and group workshops turn concepts into practice; members can become champions.
02OrganiseSmall, self-governing savings-and-lending groups, the cajas, build the first layer of trust.
03PoolCajas pool equipment, inputs and buying power that no single farmer can reach.
04AccessOrganised communities negotiate fair, transparent terms with buyers.
ESKALA's role

Connecting every partner in the network.

Communities save, lend, self-govern and pool resources. Buyers offer fairer terms and transparent payment. ESKALA coaches, invests, negotiates, builds the network and keeps everyone accountable.

The principle

ESKALA's job is to build and scale collective agency, not dependence on ESKALA.

Governance

Building trust through structure.

For the model to scale ethically, governance has to scale with the capital. Zones govern regional blocks, centres coordinate the cajas in an area, and each caja governs its own savings, lending and duties.

Design principle

Centralise accountability where it creates value. Decentralise decisions where local knowledge matters.

What we learned

Change the relationship around capital, not just the loan.

Individual borrower→Collective financial actor
Capital as the intervention→Capital plus capability
Lender relationship→Community network
Market access→Market leverage
Growth through dependency→Growth through local agency

For farmers

Access to knowledge, shared resources and collective market relationships.

For the system

More value redistributed, while building social capital and local ownership.

Overall

Farmers become a collective financial actor rather than individual borrowers.

What next

Holding the momentum.

The real challenge will be scaling trust, capability and collective power while building independence.

Economics

Earn from value created, not debt: buyer fees and a share of negotiated price uplift, plus capped rates. ESKALA wins only when farmers do.

Digital equity

Offline-first and group-based: the caja ledger stays the source of truth and digital just mirrors it. No farmer needs a phone to take part.

Governance at scale

Federate, don't centralise. Standardise the interfaces, not the decisions, so more cajas mean more collective power, not more admin.

Eyewa

Reconnecting a fragmented eyewear journey to lift order value and loyalty for a fast-growing Gulf retailer.

RoleResearch, strategy and design
TeamHarsh Vatsa, Joanna Varghese, Clara Chen
Timeline5-week design sprint, 2025
SettingEyewa, Dubai
The brief

A live strategy challenge from a fast-scaling retailer.

Parsons Design Sprints pairs studio teams with outside partners on real strategic problems. Our partner was Eyewa, a Middle East eyewear retailer growing fast across the Gulf. We were briefed directly by their Chief Strategy Officer and Strategy Lead.

$100MSeries C raise
250stores planned
Verticalintegrated operating model
Problem

Value leaks between channels, and loyalty doesn't build.

76%of business is offline
17%of sales are online
29%of online carts are bought offline
7%of customers are truly omnichannel
How might we

Connect the journey across online and offline to raise average order value and NPS, and open a new revenue stream?

Research

Discovery to design with one creative problem-solving arc.

We scoped market positioning, product experience, personas, pricing, competitive forces and the supply chain, then moved quickly from discovery to design using the Osborn–Parnes creative problem-solving process.

Insights

Four insights we built for.

The continuity gap

29% of online carts convert offline, but the handoff isn't tracked, so the sale can't be credited to the journey.

Flat order value

Mid-tier products and add-ons are missing and there's no loyalty loop, so the path from base to premium stays invisible.

The range gap

Two similarly priced house brands can't cover both value and premium. Branding should sell self-expression.

Share, not margin

Vertically integrated and well funded, Eyewa's mandate is to grab market share, not optimise margin.

Solution 1

Closing the continuity leaks: one profile, both channels.

A continuity layer so a browsing session doesn't die at the cart. It follows the customer into the store, and Eyewa finally sees it.

01Online: browse and saveCart, wishlist and prescription saved to a profile.
02Reserve and try in storeStaff pull up the online session on arrival.
03Offline: try, fit, buyThe purchase is credited to the online journey.
Solution 2

Personalisation as the order-value and loyalty engine.

A guided journey that builds confidence and basket size at the moment of decision, then remembers the customer and rewards them for coming back.

Guided fitSmart bundlesLoyalty rewards
Solution 3

Brands built around identity.

Eyewa's two house brands, 30 Sundays (trendy, versatile) and Blackout (dark, bold, edgy), are priced alike. We proposed extending the range to how customers express themselves, covering both ends of the market.

Gaze and Glint

Proposed. Accessible, functional eyeglasses for the price-first customer, at an entry price point.

Cheval

Proposed. Better-built, design-led eyeglasses for the customer who'll pay for quality, at a luxury price point.

Collaborative Consumption

Mapping the access economy to understand what we gain, and what we lose, when access replaces ownership.

What quietly went away without anyone noticing? Ownership.

We used to pay money and then the thing actually belonged to us. Now everything is rented, leased or sold "as a service": music, software, even printer ink. We spend and spend, and in the end we hold nothing in our hands.

This was a research project for the course New Economies and Societies at Parsons. I mapped the access economy to understand what we gain and lose when access replaces ownership: who the actors are, how platform, gig, access, sharing and peer economies divide up labour and markets, and which companies sit in each. I also looked at the trade-off of membership and subscription models, which widen access but bring customer fatigue, rising prices and underused value, and ended with practical questions about how to engage with this economy without becoming more vulnerable to it.

Speculative design · Concept

Winks

What if you could revisit any moment from your past, and learn from it?

Team
Yuze Li, Arpit Ahluwalia, Swasti Solanki, Harsh Vatsa

Winner, Parsons School Prize at Figma Build 2025, Figma’s first national design competition, chosen from 60+ teams and 170+ students.

Winks is a speculative superpower, presented as the onboarding deck for "Winks Club, the universe's most advanced superhero academy". Wink three times and a holographic Memory Disk lets you scroll back through time, filter by tags or emotion, and dive into a moment.

The concept is about learning, not nostalgia. After every dive you log a reflection, which builds a personal growth timeline, and an efficiency tracker shows the skills you gained. Because the power is risky, the design builds in limits: a yearly allowance of memory tokens, cooldowns before re-entry, memories that lock after two visits, and safeguards against traumatic triggers, addiction to reliving, and people appearing in shared memories without consent.
